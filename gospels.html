Practice two

How to Read the Gospels

The Gospels are how we come to know Jesus: what He said, what He did, how He treated people, and what He asks of us.

If you want to know Christ, read the Gospels.

Not to master them. Not to be able to discuss them. Simply to spend time with Him and let His words work on you.

They are short. All four together are about the length of a paperback novel, and any one of them can be read in an afternoon, though that is not what I would recommend.

Part one

The four Gospels

16 chapters · the shortest

Mark

Mark moves quickly. He tells the story plainly and urgently, and he does not slow down to explain much.

Start here. It is the shortest, and once you have read it the other three make more sense.

28 chapters

Matthew

Matthew writes for people who knew the Hebrew Scriptures, and he keeps pointing back to them: this is the one you were promised.

His Gospel is built around five great blocks of teaching. The first is the Sermon on the Mount, chapters five through seven, and there is little in Scripture more concentrated than those three chapters.

24 chapters

Luke

Luke is a careful writer who gathered what he could from those who were there. He notices the people others pass over: the poor, foreigners, women, the disreputable.

Many of the parables you half remember are his, including the Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son.

If Mark feels too spare, read Luke instead.

21 chapters

John

John was written last and does something different. There are fewer events and much longer conversations, and he returns again and again to a handful of images: bread, light, water, a vine.

It is beautiful, and it is harder to begin with, because it assumes you already know the story it is unfolding. Read it third or fourth.

Part two

How to read it

Read slowly. This is not information to get through. It is a Person to meet.

A short passage, five or ten verses, is enough. The Church has handed down a simple way of praying with Scripture, and it has four movements.

  1. 1
    Read

    Slowly, and out loud if you can. Then read it a second time. The second reading is usually where something catches.

  2. 2
    Stay

    Something will stand out: a word, a phrase, a detail that seems strange. Stop there instead of pressing on. Ask what it means, and what it would have been like to be standing there.

  3. 3
    Speak to God

    Say something back to Him about what you have read. This is the movement people skip, and it is the one that turns reading into prayer.

  4. 4
    Rest

    Sit quietly for a minute without trying to produce anything. It will feel like wasting time. It is not.

When you don't understand it

Mark it and move on. You are not being examined. Some of it will open up years from now, and some passages remain difficult for everyone, including people who have studied them their whole lives.

If something troubles you, ask a priest. That is what he is there for.

Which Bible

Any Catholic edition is fine. The Revised Standard Version, Catholic Edition reads well aloud. The New American Bible is what you will hear at Mass in the United States, so it has the advantage of sounding familiar.

A paper copy is easier than a phone, mostly because a phone has other things on it.

Part three

A first month

Sixteen chapters of Mark, about ten minutes a day, with room to miss days. At the end you will have read an entire Gospel.

Week one

Mark 1–4

Jesus begins His ministry and calls His first disciples.

Read one chapter a day for four days. Use the other days to return to whatever struck you.

Week two

Mark 5–8

The miracles, and the growing question of who He is.

Chapter 8 is the turning point of the whole Gospel. Slow down there.

Week three

Mark 9–12

He turns toward Jerusalem, and the teaching grows harder.
Week four

Mark 13–16

The last week, the Cross, and the empty tomb.

Read 14 and 15 in one sitting if you can.

Then what

Luke next, for the same reason you would read a second account of anything: the same events, seen by someone who noticed different things. Then Matthew, and John last.

Last thing

Two things worth knowing

Ten minutes a day is better than an hour on Sunday. A short passage each morning changes how the day reads. A long session once a week is a lecture you attend.

You are not reading alone. The Church reads through the Gospels at Mass across a three-year cycle, so the passage you hear on Sunday is being heard by Catholics everywhere that same day. Reading on your own is meant to feed that, not replace it.

Ignorance of Scripture is ignorance of Christ. St. Jerome
