Practice two

How to Read the Gospels

Four people wrote down the same life for four different audiences. None of them was trying to be comprehensive, and all of them assumed you would read slowly.

Part one

The four books

They are short. All four together are about the length of a paperback novel, and any one of them can be read in an afternoon, though that is not the recommendation.

16 chapters · the shortest

Mark

Fast, blunt, and almost breathless. The word usually translated "immediately" turns up over thirty times. Mark is the earliest of the four and reads like someone telling you an urgent story they have not stopped to smooth out. No infancy narrative; it opens with a grown man walking into a river.

Start here. It is the shortest and the most immediate, and once you have read it the other three make more sense.

28 chapters

Matthew

Written for people who knew the Hebrew scriptures well, and constantly pointing back at them: this is the one you were told about. Organized around five big blocks of teaching, the first of which is the Sermon on the Mount, chapters five to seven, which is as concentrated as anything in the book.

If you only ever read three chapters of the Bible, most people would tell you to make it those.

24 chapters · the best storytelling

Luke

A careful writer, working from interviews, addressing an outsider. Luke keeps noticing the people the others pass over: the poor, foreigners, women, the disreputable. The parables you already half-know are mostly his, including the Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son.

If Mark feels too spare, read Luke instead. It is the warmest of the four.

21 chapters · different in kind

John

Written last and doing something else entirely. Fewer events, much longer conversations, and a habit of circling one image for a whole chapter: bread, light, water, a vine. It opens not with a birth but with the beginning of everything.

Beautiful, and harder to start with, because it assumes you already know the story it is interpreting. Read it third or fourth, not first.

Part two

How to actually read it

The mistake is reading it like information. It is short enough to finish in a week and dense enough to occupy a lifetime, and reading it fast gets you the first without the second.

The old method has four movements and no equipment. It takes about ten minutes and works on a passage of five or ten verses, not a whole chapter.

  1. 1
    Read

    Slowly, and out loud if you are somewhere you can. Then read it a second time. The second reading is where you start noticing things, so skipping it skips the point.

  2. 2
    Stay

    Something will catch: a word, a phrase, a detail that seems odd. Stop there rather than pressing on. Ask what it is doing, why it was worth writing down, what it would have been like to be standing there.

  3. 3
    Talk

    Say something back. Out loud or not. This is the movement people skip, and it is the one that turns reading into prayer; without it you are studying a text about God rather than speaking to him.

  4. 4
    Stop talking

    Sit there for a minute without doing anything. No agenda, nothing to produce. This feels like wasting time and is not.

What to do when it is confusing

Mark it and move on. You are not being tested. Some of it will open up on the fourth reading years from now, and some of it stays strange for everyone, including people who have studied it their whole lives.

Which translation

Any Catholic edition is fine. The Revised Standard Version, Catholic Edition is accurate and reads well aloud; the New American Bible is what you will hear at Mass in the United States, so it has the advantage of sounding familiar. A real paper copy beats an app, mostly because a phone has other things on it.

Part three

A first month

Sixteen chapters of Mark, about ten minutes a day, with room to miss days. At the end of it you will have read an entire Gospel, which most people never do.

Week one

Mark 1–4

He appears, gathers a handful of people, and the crowds start.

One chapter a day, four days. Use the other days to reread whatever struck you.

Week two

Mark 5–8

The miracles, and the growing sense that nobody quite understands what is happening.

Chapter 8 is the hinge of the whole book. Slow down there.

Week three

Mark 9–12

He turns toward Jerusalem, and the teaching gets harder.
Week four

Mark 13–16

The last week, the trial, the cross, and an ending so abrupt that scribes kept adding to it.

Read 14 and 15 in one sitting if you can. They were meant to be heard that way.

Then what

Luke, for the same reason you would read a second account of anything: it is the same events by someone who noticed different things. Then Matthew, then John.

Last thing

Two things worth knowing

Ten minutes beats an hour on Sunday. The daily part is doing more work than the length. A short passage every morning changes how the day reads; a long session once a week is a lecture you attend.

It is meant to be read in company. The Church reads through most of these at Mass across a three-year cycle, so the passage you hear on Sunday is being heard by everyone else in the room at the same time. Reading on your own is not a substitute for that, and it is not supposed to be.

Ignorance of Scripture is ignorance of Christ. St. Jerome
