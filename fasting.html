Practice five

Fasting

Fasting is not a diet and not a test of willpower. It is a way of turning toward God with your body as well as your soul.

Part one

What it is for

Christ fasted. He also assumed that we would.

When He taught about it in the Sermon on the Mount, He did not say "if you fast." He said "when you fast." The Church has kept that practice ever since.

Fasting does something simple and uncomfortable: it shows you what you actually depend on. You can tell yourself almost anything about your own freedom. Going without food for a day tells you the truth in a few hours.

That is not the whole of it. Scripture joins fasting to prayer again and again, as though the two strengthen each other. People who practice both say that they do.

And what you do not spend on yourself can be given to someone else. Fasting and almsgiving belong together; the Church has always taught them side by side.

Part two

What the Church asks, and what is left to you

It is worth being clear about which is which. A vague sense of obligation is heavier to carry than a real one, and it is easy to confuse a good custom with a binding law.

Required

The days the Church binds

Ash Wednesday and Good Friday are days of fasting. This means one full meal, plus two smaller ones that together do not equal a full meal, and no eating between. In the Latin Church this obligation binds those from 18 through 59.

Ash Wednesday, Good Friday, and all the Fridays of Lent are days of abstinence from meat. This binds Catholics from 14 onward.

If you are pregnant or nursing, ill, elderly, or your work makes fasting genuinely unsafe, you are excused. That is the Church's own provision, not a loophole.

Every Friday of the year remains a day of penance. In the United States, the bishops permit Catholics to substitute another act of penance or charity for abstaining from meat outside of Lent. What is not offered is doing nothing; Friday is the day Christ died.

Before receiving Communion, the Church asks that we take nothing but water and medicine for one hour.

Freely chosen

Everything else

Beyond those days, fasting is voluntary. Catholics have always fasted more than the minimum, but that is a free offering rather than a requirement, and nobody is in sin for not doing it.

If you take something on, take it on freely and tell God why. And if your health, your work, or your state in life makes it unwise, do something else instead. Extra fasting that harms you is not pleasing to God.

If you are unsure whether a particular fast is wise for you, ask a priest. That is an ordinary question and he will have heard it many times.

Part three

How to begin

Begin with one Friday. Not Lent, not a rule for the rest of your life. One Friday coming up.

  1. 1
    Decide in advance

    Choose the day before it arrives. A fast decided on Tuesday is a fast. One decided at noon on Friday, when you happen not to be hungry, is not much.

  2. 2
    Choose something specific

    You might go without meat, or eat nothing between meals, or take only bread and water until dinner. Vague intentions quietly collapse in the afternoon, because there is nothing definite to have broken.

  3. 3
    Offer it for someone

    Fast for a particular person or a particular intention, and tell God so when you begin. It stops being an exercise in endurance and becomes a prayer.

  4. 4
    Give away what it saved

    Give the money, or the time you did not spend on lunch. This is the part most easily dropped, and it is the part that turns the fast outward toward your neighbor.

  5. 5
    Keep it quiet

    Christ is direct about this: do not announce it. Fast in a way that only your Father sees.

When it goes badly

You may find yourself irritable, and you will notice how much of an ordinary day is arranged around small comforts. That is not the fast failing. That is the fast doing what it does.

If you break it, eat the meal and begin again next Friday. A broken fast is something that happened on a Friday. It is not a verdict on you.

Fasting is the soul of prayer, and mercy is the life of fasting. Let no one try to separate them; they cannot be divided. St. Peter Chrysologus
