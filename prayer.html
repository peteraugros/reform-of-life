Practice six

Prayer

The Rosary gave you words. This is what happens when you run out of them, which is where most of the real thing turns out to be.

Part one

Talking plainly

Set prayers are scaffolding, and scaffolding is not an insult; it holds you up on the days you have nothing. But if every word you have ever said to God was written by somebody else in the fourth century, something is missing.

So: say the actual thing. Out loud, in the car, in the words you would use with a friend who could take it. "I do not know what to do about this." "I am angry with you about her." "Thank you, that was not nothing."

People hesitate because it feels presumptuous or because it feels like talking to the ceiling. Both feelings are normal and neither is an argument. The Psalms, which are the Church's own prayer book, contain complaint, fury, and open accusation. Nothing you are carrying is more irreverent than what is already in there.

Part two

The Examen

Ten minutes at the end of the day, five movements, and it is the single most portable practice in this whole site. Ignatius told his order that if they had to drop everything else, they should keep this.

  1. 1
    Give thanks

    Name two or three specific things from today. Specific, not general; the coffee, the phone call, the fact the thing you dreaded was smaller than expected.

  2. 2
    Ask for light

    Ask to see the day as it actually was rather than as you have already filed it. Self-knowledge on your own steam runs about sixty percent accurate and heavily edited.

  3. 3
    Walk back through it

    Go from morning to now. You are not judging it, only replaying it. Where was there life in it, and where did something go flat or cold? Those two questions do most of the work.

  4. 4
    Face the one thing

    There will be a moment you would rather skip. Say it plainly and ask for mercy. This is a minute of your evening, not an inquisition.

  5. 5
    Look at tomorrow

    One thing you can see coming, handed over in advance.

Seven nights of this and you will start noticing patterns you have been living inside for years without seeing. That is the whole promise, and it is a large one.

Part three

The holy hour

Sitting in front of the Blessed Sacrament for an hour, doing essentially nothing. Most parishes have adoration at some point in the week, and many have a chapel open longer than you would guess.

Bring nothing, or bring the Gospel you are reading. There is no technique. You sit there, your mind wanders enormously, you come back, and the hour ends. It will feel like a waste of time for the first several attempts.

The argument for doing it anyway is that almost everyone who has kept at this says the same thing: the hour that felt emptiest is the one they later trace things back to. That is not evidence you can hand to someone. It is the reason to try it more than once.

Last thing

When nothing happens

It will stop feeling like anything. Weeks of it, sometimes much longer, where prayer is flat and you suspect you are talking to yourself.

Every serious writer on this describes the same stretch, and they are unanimous about the response: do not change the practice while you are in it. Keep the same time, the same length. Deciding to pray less because it has stopped being enjoyable is deciding to pray only when it pays, and that is a different arrangement than the one on offer.

Distraction is not failure either. The mind wanders, you come back. Coming back is the prayer, exactly as it was on the beads.

Prayer is nothing else than being on terms of friendship with God. St. Teresa of Avila
